# Write the policy for the tools people already use

At my previous employer, we tried to ban AI tools. It lasted about a fortnight.

## Bans move the risk, they do not remove it

A survey of 2,000 people by Acme Research found that 61% of office workers use generative AI tools at least weekly [UNVERIFIED]. When a company blocks those tools, the work does not stop. It moves to personal phones and home laptops, where nobody can see it.

Most companies still treat AI tools like a fire alarm: they react when it rings. That approach made sense when the tools were few. It does not now.

## The gap is the policy, not the tools

The same survey found that 38% of people have used an AI tool their employer has not approved [UNVERIFIED], and only 29% say their employer has a written policy [UNVERIFIED]. That is the real exposure: use without rules.

## Govern what is already happening

Start with visibility. List the tools in use, decide which data may go into them, and give people an approved option for each common task. A ban is a policy for the tools you can see.

What did your organisation do first: block, or write the rules?
